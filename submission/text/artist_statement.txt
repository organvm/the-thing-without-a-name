The 2017 piece was a cubist study made by feeling. I cut where the edges wanted to go, but the fragments were never only flat pieces: each raw photograph was a view into the same room from the same camera position. Danse begins there, with the conviction that a photograph records both an instant and the place from which it was seen.

The tracked corpus is deliberately specific. It contains 161 registered raw photographs from 20 June 2017 and one unregistered archival composite made afterward. The current reconstruction draws from 76 of those photographs and the composite itself. That self-reference is kept visible rather than disguised, while new generated cuts use only the registered camera sources. Every delivered visual fragment must remain accountable to a declared record.

The ScreenDance capture lets the image strike the score before the engine begins. Across the first thirty seconds, all 161 photographs flash forward and backward on a sixfold subdivision of the Delibes pulse. The hand-edited 2017 composite then takes over with an almost imperceptible drift before dissolving into the shared engine at one minute. From there, the camera leaves the original viewpoint slowly, poses recur as authored motifs, stillness lasts for a complete phrase, and the climax grows from variation rather than erasing the field with a new seed.

The title comes from Stephen King's description in Danse Macabre of a figure assembled from other bodies. The monsters pictured on the apartment wall were not added as symbols later; they were already part of the room. The film ends on a black title and credit card. That capture is finite, but the shared engine from which it is taken remains larger than the recording.
